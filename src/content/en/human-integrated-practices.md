## Overview: Building Research for the Future

SHIHAB began with a simple question: What are astronauts missing?

At first, this question felt distant from the research we encounter in our everyday lives. But as we explored the challenges of human spaceflight, we realized that the future we were imagining is no longer science fiction. Space agencies around the world are preparing for missions that will take humans farther from Earth and for longer periods than ever before. Behind these missions are not only explorers, but scientists working to expand our understanding of life: from how the human body adapts to space to how biological systems respond to environments beyond Earth.

As scientists ourselves, this changed the way we saw the problem. If astronauts are helping us push the boundaries of research, how can research help them?

This question became the foundation of SHIHAB and of our Integrated Human Practices (IHP). Developing a system for on-demand biomanufacturing in space requires much more than engineering microorganisms in the laboratory. It requires understanding the people who may use it, the environments in which it must operate, the regulations that could govern it, and the broader consequences of introducing such a technology.

Through more than 30 activities and stakeholder engagements, we explored SHIHAB from multiple perspectives, learning from astronauts, space medicine specialists, researchers, industry representatives, regulatory experts, and the wider community. Their experiences helped us question our assumptions, identify limitations, and make decisions about the biological design, hardware, safety, implementation, and communication of our project.

These conversations also pushed us to look beyond space. The need for local and reliable access to therapeutics is not unique to astronauts. Remote communities, disaster-affected regions, and other settings with disrupted or limited supply chains can face similar challenges in accessing essential medicines. What began as a question about medical autonomy beyond Earth therefore became a broader exploration of how decentralized biomanufacturing could contribute to healthcare both in space and on Earth.

Our Human Practices journey was not separate from SHIHAB, it became part of how we built it. By continuously connecting stakeholder needs, scientific evidence, societal perspectives, and our own experimental work, we aimed to transform an ambitious idea into a more responsible, relevant, and realistic solution.

## Understanding Our Impact and Responsibility

For most of our team, applying research to space exploration was entirely new territory. As we began developing SHIHAB, we quickly realized that working in an unfamiliar and rapidly evolving field also came with a significant responsibility. This meant that before moving into design decisions, we first had to ground ourselves in the realities of space healthcare and the constraints of the environment we were working toward. We needed to understand the context in which our ideas could operate, recognize the limitations of our own expertise, and consider the risks and consequences our decisions could have.

Our first questions soon led to many others. What do astronauts actually need? Would producing therapeutics in space be feasible and safe? What would it take for such a system to move beyond the laboratory? And who should have a voice in shaping it? Each question helped us identify knowledge gaps and guided us toward people whose expertise and experiences could challenge our assumptions and inform our decisions.

Our goal was therefore not simply to showcase SHIHAB or seek validation. We wanted to establish meaningful dialogues capable of changing the way we thought about the problem and, when necessary, the project itself. Each engagement was selected with a purpose, bringing perspectives from different disciplines and communities into the development of SHIHAB.

## Our ORBIT Framework

To guide this process, we developed the ORBIT framework, a continuous cycle connecting stakeholder engagement with project development:

1. **O — Observe the Challenge** — Understand the healthcare, biological, and logistical challenges of space exploration.
2. **R — Reach Stakeholders** — Engage astronauts, researchers, space medicine specialists, industry, regulatory experts and communities.
3. **B — Build with Insights** — Translate stakeholder knowledge into concrete decisions for SHIHAB.
4. **I — Iterate Responsibly** — Reassess assumptions, limitations, risks and design choices as new perspectives emerge.
5. **T — Translate the Impact** — Extend what we learn beyond the laboratory, toward space exploration, Earth applications, and society.

Rather than following these steps only once, ORBIT became an iterative process. New insights generated new questions, new questions connected us with new stakeholders, and those interactions continuously shaped the direction of SHIHAB.

## Defining Our Stakeholders

As SHIHAB evolved, we identified the people and communities who could inform, challenge, evaluate, regulate, use, or be affected by our solution. We grouped our stakeholders according to the perspective they brought to the project and asked two questions for each group:

- What do we need to learn from them?
- How could their perspective influence SHIHAB?

| Stakeholder group | What we need to learn from them | How it could influence SHIHAB |
|---|---|---|
| Astronauts & Space Medicine | What medical problems matter?<br>How are medicines currently managed?<br>What would astronauts actually trust or use? | Therapeutic choice<br>Usability<br>Autonomy<br>Safety requirements |
| Scientists & Technical Experts | Which organisms could work under space-associated stresses?<br>Which microorganisms and toolkits can be used for our purpose?<br>What are the major limitations of our research? | Host selection<br>Stress engineering<br>Experimental design<br>Testing |
| Regulatory, Safety & Ethics | How could an engineered microorganism producing a therapeutic be safely used?<br>What regulations apply? | Containment<br>Biosafety<br>Quality controls<br>Responsible design and production |
| Humanitarian context | Could decentralized biomanufacturing be useful where medicine supply chains are disrupted?<br>What additional requirements would these environments introduce? | Disaster-zone application<br>Broader accessibility to our platform<br>Remote deployment |
| Community | What does society understand about space biotechnology?<br>What knowledge gaps, concerns or misconceptions exist? | Educational materials<br>Communication Strategy<br>Space Academy |

## Our Journey

1. **Exploring The Unknown** — What do we think the problem is?
2. **Listening to Astronauts & Space Medicine** — Is this really the problem?
3. **Exploring Biological Solutions** — Could synthetic biology address it?
4. **From Concept to Implementation** — What does it take to bring biomanufacturing beyond the laboratory?
5. **Looking Back at Earth** — Can the challenges of space reveal solutions for healthcare on Earth?
6. **Engaging Society** — How does society understand and perceive this technology?
