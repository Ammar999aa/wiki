## An international team in Saudi Arabia

Inequality and discrimination can limit access to scientific opportunities and prevent people from contributing their talents and perspectives. At SHIHAB, we sought to address these barriers through inclusive recruitment, respect for privacy, and fair opportunities to take on leadership roles. Our team selection process focused on applicants’ motivation and relevant skills. To protect their privacy, we requested only information related to their potential responsibilities, without asking them to disclose personal characteristics such as sexual orientation or nationality. The same principle guided the selection of working committee leaders: responsibilities were assigned based on members’ participation, commitment, and performance throughout the project.

Our 28 team members represent more than 10 countries, including Saudi Arabia, Peru, Colombia, India, the United States, Kazakhstan, China, Türkiye, and Singapore. Working together at KAUST in Saudi Arabia, we brought different cultures, faiths, disciplines, and languages into a shared scientific mission.

## Bringing different disciplines into synthetic biology

Synthetic biology brings together questions that no single discipline can answer alone. Yet students outside the biological sciences may struggle to see a place for themselves in the field. Within SHIHAB, we wanted members to recognize how their knowledge could shape the project and feel confident contributing beyond the boundaries of their degree. Our team brings together backgrounds in **biology, bioengineering, computer science, and mechanical engineering**. Working together required us to explain ideas across disciplines, question assumptions, and learn from colleagues whose approaches differed from our own. This exchange connected our work on microbial chassis and protective genes with computational modeling and bioreactor development.

For us, inclusion also means recognizing whose work receives visibility and credit. Experiments, code, hardware, coordination, and communication all shape what the team can achieve. Valuing these contributions means acknowledging the people behind them and ensuring that no single form of expertise defines who belongs in synthetic biology.

Every member of our team speaks more than one language. This shared multilingualism gives us opportunities to communicate with people across different linguistic communities.

Language can affect whether students feel comfortable exploring a scientific topic or asking questions. To address this barrier, we developed educational materials in English and Arabic for students, alongside Spanish-language materials for audiences in other countries.

By providing resources in these languages, we aimed to make our activities more approachable and create more opportunities for people to engage with synthetic biology. Our multilingual approach connects the international nature of our team with practical efforts to broaden access to scientific learning.

### Science in a language students can call their own

For Arabic speakers, this language barrier is especially visible. Most synthetic biology resources exist only in English, and the field barely appears in secondary school curricula in Saudi Arabia and the wider MENA region. We designed our Arabic materials in Arabic from the start, rather than translating English content as an afterthought.

### Knowledge that travels through communities

In addition to Arabic, we have developed materials in Spanish in collaboration with Kallpa Greenz, a leading company in the construction of smart greenhouses across three distinct Peruvian climate zones (the Amazon rainforest, the coast, and the Andes). Working alongside rural and peri-urban communities, the initiative also provides training for those who operate these facilities.

Young volunteers from Kallpa received training on GMOs and synthetic biology, utilizing a “train-the-trainer” (cascade) model. The program primarily targets rural agricultural communities in Peru.
