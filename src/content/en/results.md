## Summary

We engineered three microbial chassis for enhanced stress resilience and produced a range of recombinant proteins useful for space missions. In short, our key results are summarized as follows:

<div class="card-grid stack">

<div>

#### Dsup integration · three chassis

Successful genomic integration and expression of wild-type Dsup (WT Dsup) in _Bacillus subtilis_, _Pichia pastoris_, and _Saccharomyces cerevisiae_, confirmed by Western blot detection of the myc-tagged version of the gene.

</div>

<div>

#### Improved Dsup variant

Successful genomic integration and expression of a modified Dsup variant with enhanced nuclear localization in _P. pastoris_.

</div>

<div>

#### Radiation resistance from Dsup

Demonstrated that WT Dsup expression enhances the resistance of _B. subtilis_ and _P. pastoris_ to UV and X-ray irradiation compared to unmodified cells.

</div>

<div>

#### Nuclear localization improves protection

Demonstrated that the improved Dsup variant with enhanced nuclear localization provides greater protection against irradiation-induced damage in _P. pastoris_ compared to WT Dsup.

</div>

<div>

#### Four payloads purified from _P. pastoris_

Achieved recombinant expression and purification of two antifreeze proteins (FfIBP and LeIBP) and two nanobodies (anti-PDL1 Nb and Nb1_gbHSV) in _P. pastoris_, obtaining sufficient protein purity and concentration through methanol-induced expression and affinity purification using the ÄKTA HisTrap system.

</div>

<div>

#### Secretion efficiency

Conducted a comparative analysis of intracellular and extracellular protein levels in _P. pastoris_ expressing recombinant antifreeze protein with an α-factor secretion signal to evaluate protein secretion efficiency.

</div>

<div>

#### Payload production in _B. subtilis_

Achieved recombinant expression and purification of the secreted antifreeze protein FfIBP in _B. subtilis_, achieving sufficient protein purity and concentration through IPTG-induced expression and affinity purification using the ÄKTA HisTrap system.

</div>

<div>

#### Dsup does not cost yield

Confirmed that genomic integration of Dsup under a constitutively active promoter does not significantly impact payload protein production in _B. subtilis_.

</div>

<div>

#### Sporulation and recovery

Induced sporulation of genetically modified _B. subtilis_ and subsequently demonstrated GFP production from the resulting spore-derived cultures.

</div>

<div>

#### Induction optimized · _P. pastoris_

Optimized methanol induction conditions for mCherry expression in _P. pastoris_.

</div>

<div>

#### Induction optimized · _B. subtilis_

Evaluated the effects of different IPTG concentrations on GFP expression in genetically engineered _B. subtilis_ 168 to optimize induction conditions.

</div>

<div>

#### UV-C survival · _B. subtilis_

Demonstrated that Dsup-expressing _B. subtilis_ retains one to two orders of magnitude more colony-forming cells than wild type at 12 mJ/m² of UV-C, consistent across three technical replicates.

</div>

<div>

#### X-ray tolerance · _B. subtilis_

Showed that Dsup-expressing _B. subtilis_ maintains its optical density after X-ray doses above 1000 Gy, where wild-type cultures fall below their starting density.

</div>

<div>

#### UV-C survival · _P. pastoris_

Observed that Dsup-expressing _P. pastoris_ retains growth across all six serial dilutions up to 24 mJ/m² of UV-C, while the control strain does not.

</div>

<div>

#### X-ray dose response · three chassis

Characterized the X-ray dose response of _B. subtilis_, _S. cerevisiae_ and _P. pastoris_ up to 2000 Gy, establishing the working dose range for each organism and ranking their relative radioresistance.

</div>

<div>

#### Resazurin viability assay

Established and optimized a resazurin-based viability assay for both bacterial and yeast chassis, defining the cell density and reading window required for reliable measurement.

</div>

<div>

#### Spot dilution assays

Implemented spot dilution assays as a quantitative survival readout, resolving differences in viability of up to four orders of magnitude between conditions.

</div>

</div>

<summary class="details-summary"><i>S. cerevisiae</i> recombineering</summary>

<div class="details-body">

Lithium acetate (LiAc)-mediated heat-shock transformation was used to introduce recombinant DNA into _S. cerevisiae_. <summary class="details-summary"><i>B. subtilis</i> transformations</summary>

<div class="details-body">

Natural competence-mediated transformation was used to introduce recombinant DNA into _B. subtilis_. A simplified protocol utilizing a single SP medium was employed to induce natural competence, eliminating the need for continuous OD600 monitoring and multiple competence-inducing media. Plasmid DNA was added at the beginning of the competence development step to facilitate DNA uptake. After incubation, cells were transferred to LB medium for recovery, allowing sufficient time for the expression of antibiotic resistance markers before plating on selective agar to isolate transformants.

<div class="caption">

**Figure 5.** Successful _B. subtilis_ transformation results.

By using this protocol, we achieved _B. subtilis_ transformation. The following colonies were then restreaked on a plate with antibiotic and 1% starch containing LB plate. Since the pBS1C plasmid is an integrative plasmid and integrates at the starch hydrolysis operon, a successful insertion should not hydrolyze starch on the plate, which is checked by staining the plate with iodine solution. A clear halo was seen around the negative control colony with the pHT-ads plasmid, an episomal plasmid, suggesting a successful insertion.

A Dsup-inserted _B. subtilis_ strain was created using this method. The colonies were restreaked on a new antibiotic plate and one of the verified colonies was used again along with wild type to make strains containing episomal plasmids for expressing GFPmut2 (iGEMG1) and FfIBP (iGEMBF1). AmyQ-FfIBP (iGEMBF2), AmyQ-LeIBP (iGEMBL2) and AmyQ nanobody1 (iGEMBN1) constructs were also transformed into wild-type _Bacillus_, using the same method.

Previously, we tested other _B. subtilis_ transformation protocols, including electroporation. However, they did not yield positive results. Hence, we decided to stick to the protocol that was used to achieve the results in Figure 5.

Ten-fold serial dilutions of wild-type and Dsup-expressing _B. subtilis_ were spotted after UV-C exposure (Figure 9). Figures 12 and 13 illustrate the effects of increasing UV radiation doses (0–300 J/cm²) on the metabolic activity and relative viability of wild-type (WT) and Dsup-expressing _B. subtilis_. Figure 12 demonstrates that Dsup-expressing cells generally maintained higher resazurin reduction levels following UV irradiation compared to WT cells. At 60 J/cm², Dsup-expressing cells exhibited approximately 148% dye reduction compared to 120% in WT cells. At higher radiation doses, the difference became more pronounced, particularly at 240 J/cm², where Dsup-expressing cells maintained approximately 110% reduction compared to only 55% in WT cells.

Figure 13 further supports this observation by presenting resazurin-based relative viability normalized to each strain's non-irradiated control. At 60 J/cm², Dsup-expressing cells exhibited approximately 121% relative viability, compared to 84% in WT cells. At 240 J/cm², WT relative viability decreased to approximately 39%, whereas Dsup-expressing cells maintained approximately 90%. Even at the highest tested dose of 300 J/cm², Dsup-expressing cells retained approximately 89% relative viability compared to 68% in WT cells.

### X-ray irradiation of _B. subtilis_

Cultures of wild-type and Dsup-expressing _B. subtilis_ were irradiated with X-rays and followed by OD₆₀₀ for 60 min in wells without resazurin, each series normalised to its own starting value (Figure 14). Unirradiated cultures reached 2.3 times the initial density in the wild type and 2.7 times in Dsup, and at 500 Gy growth was largely arrested in both. Above that dose the wild type ended below where it started, at 0.86, 0.73 and 0.70 of the initial OD at 1000, 1500 and 2000 Gy, whereas Dsup held at or above 1.0 throughout. Since neither strain divides above 500 Gy, Dsup is not keeping cells growing under irradiation but preventing the loss of density that the wild type shows, which points to cell lysis rather than arrested division.

### UV radiation of _P. pastoris_

<div class="photo-frame">

![Spot dilution assay of P. pastoris Dsup, IPD2 and nanobody control after UV irradiation](https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/results-2/fig-15.avif)

</div>

<div class="caption">

**Figure 15.** Spot dilution assay of _P. pastoris_ after UV irradiation at 0, 60, 120, 180, 240 and 300 J/m². Each row corresponds to one dose and each spot to a ten-fold dilution, in three technical replicates. Strains shown are Dsup, IPD2 and the nanobody control. The fraction at the end of each row gives the number of dilutions showing growth.

</div>

Dsup, the improved Dsup version (labelled IPD2) and the nanobody control strain of _P. pastoris_ were exposed to UV-C and plated as ten-fold serial dilutions (Figure 15). Both Dsup-expressing strains grew through all six dilutions at every dose up to 240 J/m² in all three replicates, matching their unirradiated controls. The control strain grew to five or six dilutions without irradiation and to six at 60 J/m², then declined steadily to three or four dilutions by 240 J/m², a loss of two to three orders of magnitude.

Beyond the number of dilutions, the spots themselves carry information about growth density. At low doses the first four to five spots of each row are confluent, and the series only breaks into countable colonies at the dilute end. That boundary moves in as the dose rises, and by 300 J/m² IPD2 still holds around three confluent spots per row while Dsup is down to one or two and the control to one.

The two Dsup constructs therefore tolerate doses that measurably reduce viability in the control strain. The effect appears later than in _B. subtilis_, where wild type and Dsup had already separated at 120 J/m². Between the two constructs the only difference is at 300 J/m², where the improved Dsup keeps more dilutions and more confluent growth than Dsup.

This suggests incorporating chassis-specific nuclear localization (signal simian virus 40 (SV40), in our case) can potentially lead to enhanced recognition by nuclear import receptors and increase nuclear accumulation, consequently improving Dsup's protective activity against radiation-induced DNA damage.

To further investigate the modification and validate the observed pattern, our future experiments will focus on:

- Repeating the experiments to get statistically more accurate results
- Assessing subcellular localization of WT Dsup and SV40 NLS Dsup to confirm increased accumulation of the latter within the nucleus

### X-ray dose range up to 2000 Gy

Both organisms were irradiated across a wide X-ray range to locate the doses at which survival starts to drop (Figure 16). _B. subtilis_ kept essentially the full dilution series up to 1200 Gy. The spots lose size and definition over that range, particularly from 800 Gy onwards, but the number of dilutions showing growth barely changes. The decline then happens quickly: three dilutions at 1400 and 1600 Gy, a single spot at 1800 Gy, and no growth at 2000 Gy.

_P. pastoris_ loses dilutions from the lowest doses tested, dropping from five at mock to around four between 200 and 1000 Gy and to two or three above 1200 Gy, but the loss is gradual and never collapses. Growth is still visible at 2000 Gy, the dose at which _B. subtilis_ plates nothing.

_B. subtilis_ therefore tolerates high doses with little change and then fails over a narrow window, while _P. pastoris_ is affected earlier but retains viable cells throughout the range. Neither organism shows appreciable killing below a few hundred Gy, and the subsequent comparisons between wild type and Dsup strains were run at doses well below this range.

### Setting the working cell density for the resazurin assay

Before using resazurin to compare irradiated and unirradiated cultures, the assay was set up by testing a range of starting cell densities. _B. subtilis_ was adjusted to OD600 between 0.2 and 1.0 and incubated with the dye. Two controls were included, cells without dye and medium with dye but no cells (Figure 17).

At OD600 0.2 the wells are still blue to purple, so only part of the dye has been reduced. From 0.4 the colour shifts to pink, and by 0.6 it is already saturated, with no visible difference between 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 after the same incubation.

Densities of 0.6 and above reduce all the available dye within the incubation time, which leaves no range to detect differences between conditions. OD600 0.2 leaves part of the dye unreduced, so an increase or a decrease in activity is still visible, and it was used as the working density for the irradiation experiments.

### Induction optimization of _B. subtilis_ with different IPTG concentrations

This experiment was performed to compare GFP expression from two different _B. subtilis_ strains: WT carrying the GFP construct and a modified wild type with Dsup carrying the same GFP construct. Both OD600 and GFP fluorescence were measured using a plate reader every 30 mins. A sample was also collected 2 hrs after induction for fluorescence microscopy to confirm GFP expression in the cells.

To optimize IPTG-induced recombinant protein expression in _B. subtilis_ and evaluate the potential effects of Dsup expression on cellular growth and protein production, we compared wild-type _B. subtilis_ 168 carrying a GFPmut2 expression construct with a modified strain expressing both Dsup (genomic integration) and GFP. Different IPTG concentrations were tested, while bacterial growth (OD₆₀₀) and GFP fluorescence were monitored over approximately 14 hours.

Fluorescence microscopy (Figure 18) confirmed successful production of fluorescently active GFP, as demonstrated by the intracellular green fluorescence observed in the FITC channel. Growth analysis (Figure 19) revealed that both strains maintained comparable overall growth profiles across most IPTG concentrations, reaching OD₆₀₀ values of approximately 0.8–1.0 by the end of the experiment. Although greater variability was observed in certain conditions for the Dsup-expressing strain, this may have resulted from technical factors, including medium evaporation or differences in antibiotic concentrations. Overall, these observations suggest that Dsup expression did not substantially impair bacterial growth under the tested conditions.

GFP fluorescence measurements (Figure 20) demonstrated a clear induction-dependent increase in recombinant protein expression in both strains. Following an initial adjustment period, fluorescence increased progressively over time under several IPTG concentrations, while non-induced and low-induction conditions generally exhibited substantially lower fluorescence signals. Both strains demonstrated broadly similar GFP expression profiles, suggesting that Dsup expression did not interfere with recombinant protein production.

Further analysis of maximum GFP fluorescence intensity and the maximum rate of fluorescence increase (Figure 21) revealed that the strongest GFP expression generally occurred at IPTG concentrations between 1 and 5 mM. In the Dsup-expressing strain, 2 mM IPTG produced the highest fluorescence intensity and rate of fluorescence increase, while the wild-type strain exhibited similarly strong responses at 2–5 mM IPTG. Interestingly, the Dsup-expressing strain demonstrated higher fluorescence intensity and a greater maximum fluorescence increase rate than the wild-type strain under certain conditions, particularly at 2 mM IPTG. However, additional biological replicates and statistical analysis would be required to determine the significance of these differences.

Overall, these results demonstrate that Dsup expression is compatible with IPTG-inducible recombinant protein production in _B. subtilis_, without an obvious negative impact on bacterial growth or GFP expression. This is particularly important for our project, as it provides preliminary evidence that engineering microbial chassis with radiation-protective proteins can be achieved while preserving their capacity for recombinant protein production. Further experiments, including biological replicates and fluorescence normalization to cell density, will help validate these findings and assess whether Dsup expression influences protein production efficiency.

For FfIBP expressed and secreted by _P. pastoris_, purification directly from the culture supernatant yielded a relatively pure protein preparation, without the need for additional downstream purification steps such as size-exclusion chromatography (SEC). This result is consistent with one of the major advantages of the _P. pastoris_ expression system: its ability to secrete recombinant proteins into the extracellular medium, which typically contains fewer contaminating host-cell proteins than intracellular lysates, thereby simplifying downstream purification.

To compare the efficiency of extracellular and intracellular protein recovery, we subsequently attempted to purify FfIBP from the cell pellet of the same culture. However, SDS-PAGE analysis revealed a considerably less pure protein preparation, with numerous additional protein bands corresponding to potential host-cell contaminants and no clearly enriched band corresponding to the expected molecular weight of FfIBP. In contrast, purification from the culture supernatant resulted in a much cleaner protein preparation, suggesting that extracellular secretion was an effective strategy for recovering FfIBP under our experimental conditions.

Based on these observations, we selected extracellular protein secretion as our preferred production strategy for subsequent experiments. This approach enabled the recovery of relatively pure recombinant proteins directly from the culture medium, reducing the need for additional purification steps and simplifying the overall protein production workflow.

## Antifreeze Protein Assays

### Freeze-thaw survival assay using purified FfIBP

This study evaluated the ice-binding functionality and cryoprotective potential of purified recombinant FfIBP on preserving _E. coli_. The target protein, originating from _Flavobacterium frigoris_, was secreted and affinity-purified from _Pichia pastoris_ cultures prior to functional assessment.

Harvested bacterial pellets were resuspended in Tris-NaCl buffer and supplemented with varying doses of purified FfIBP payload. After freeze-thaw challenge at −80 °C and room-temperature thaw, post-thaw viability was quantified using a spot-dilution assay alongside glycerol-supplemented positive controls and mock buffer controls.

<div class="photo-frame light">

![Freeze-thaw survival spot dilution assay for E. coli with purified FfIBP](https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/results-2/fig-43.avif)

</div>

<div class="caption">

**Figure 43.** Freeze-thaw survival assay for _E. coli_.

</div>

Figure 43 demonstrates freeze-thaw survival assay results for _E. coli_. The assay showed that purified FfIBP improved the survival of _E. coli_ cells following exposure to −80 °C. Both the no-freeze control and the positive control (15% glycerol) exhibited strong bacterial growth across the dilution series, whereas the negative control (0 mg/mL FfIBP) showed substantially reduced growth, indicating freezing-induced loss of viability.

FfIBP treatment improved bacterial survival, with 0.1 and 0.25 mg/mL showing promising protective effects. However, the response was not strictly concentration-dependent, as increasing the concentration to 0.5 mg/mL did not further improve survival. The 0.1 mg/mL concentration was selected as the preferred working concentration, as it provided visible protection while requiring a smaller volume of protein.
