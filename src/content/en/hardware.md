## The need for on-demand medicine in space

As human space exploration advances, longer-duration missions are becoming more commonplace. Astronauts on board require access to therapeutics to manage health conditions that may arise during their long-haul expeditions. However, maintaining an extensive inventory of medicines would substantially increase payload mass and hence mission costs as well <a class="cite" href="#ref-1">1</a>. Thus, the ability to produce therapeutics on-demand during flight offers a more flexible and resource-efficient solution, particularly for long-haul expeditions.

We recognize that astronauts are not typically trained to handle complex biological protocols and equipment. Hence, with the SHIHAB Bioreactor, we designed a prototype of a biomanufacturing platform consisting of a bioreactor and protein purification system that requires **low power** and is **simple to use**, **compact**, **modular** and **highly automated**. Astronauts would therefore only be required to transport the compact SHIHAB Bioreactor, together with a small inventory of "pills", to begin the production of their medicine of choice aboard their spacecraft.

### Part II: Protein purification system

Once fermentation is complete, the broth containing the secreted proteins passes through the protein purification system to yield the purified nanobodies. The system has two stages: filtration, then affinity chromatography.

### Filtration unit

**Why filtration?**

On Earth, the first step of protein purification, separating cells from the culture medium, relies on gravity. This is typically performed either through sedimentation or centrifugation; however, in microgravity, cells do not settle and liquids can behave unpredictably. Centrifuges can generate artificial gravity, but they are heavy, require large amounts of power and involve high-speed moving parts that pose vibration and safety concerns aboard a spacecraft. We therefore chose filtration, which separates components by size rather than density and works independently of gravity. Driven by pressure rather than gravity, a filtration system can be fully enclosed to prevent liquid escaping into the cabin. Our filtration system is lightweight, simple to operate, requires low-power and, once assembled by the user prior to purification, is fully automated. This makes it well suited to SHIHAB's goal of compact, astronaut-friendly biomanufacturing in space.

We designed a modular platform that allows the user to choose how many filtration stages are needed for the specific culture. Disposable filter papers of decreasing pore size sit in each module, connected by gaskets to prevent leakage. This multi-stage approach which can be rapidly assembled and disassembled retains simplicity in design while avoiding clogs.

### Affinity chromatography

Four syringe pumps dispense precise volumes of each buffer: equilibration, wash, elution and reconstitution. Each pump pairs a sterile 60 mL syringe with a stepper-motor-driven linear actuator which has been pre-calibrated for accurate dispensing. To keep a closed fluidic system, we designed the pumps to be able to be loaded and unloaded with the buffers through separate tubing lines. The syringe pump can both draw the buffer from the stock (which can be refilled easily by the user) and dispense buffer into the column.

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">3</span>Iteration 3: Rotor optimized for lower gravity</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

We redesigned the rotor geometry so that mixing does not depend on gravity, using flow simulations to guide the changes.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We printed three rotor variants that differed in blade angle, blade count and height.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

We first conducted a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation on COMSOL Multiphysics <a class="cite" href="#ref-6">6</a> to obtain an initial idea of how the three geometries affect mixing.

<div class="figure-grid single wide">
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/rotor-geometries.avif" alt="Schematic cross-sections of the three rotor geometries in the vessel with their circulation patterns: one large propeller at the bottom with a slow region above, three rows of large flat blades with separate circulation loops, and three rows of small tilted blades (current design, rotor 29 mm diameter, blades tilted 39 degrees) with one large loop along the full height"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 10. Three rotor geometries produced different circulation patterns.</strong> Schematic cross-sections of the three rotor geometries and their circulation patterns.</figcaption></figure>
</div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

We chose the three-row small tilted-blade rotor (rotor diameter 29 mm, blades tilted 39°) because it gave the most uniform mixing of the three geometries. The single large propeller at the bottom mainly stirred the lower part of the vessel and left a slow region above it, while the large flat blades split the liquid into separate circulation loops at each blade row and disturbed the liquid surface noticeably. The small tilted blades instead drove one continuous circulation loop along the full height of the liquid, allowing for better circulation of oxygen throughout the medium while keeping the design compact.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">4</span>Iteration 4: Integrating temperature control</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

Now that we had settled the mixing, it was time to start integrating the sensors. We paired a flexible heating element (rated at 100 W) with a temperature sensor read by the Arduino microcontroller, which switches the heater on and off to hold the setpoint.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We fitted the 100 W heating element around our culture vessel (filled with ~400 mL water) and submerged the waterproof DS18B20 temperature sensor in the water.

<div class="figure-grid single">
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/heating-element-100w.avif" alt="Red silicone flexible heating element (labelled 300 mm x 120 mm, 12 V, 100 W) wrapped around the glass culture vessel, with a multi-port cap on top"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 11. The 100 W heating element was too large for the vessel.</strong> The flexible heating element fitted around the culture vessel.</figcaption></figure>
</div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

We tried to heat the water from a starting ambient temperature of ~20 ºC to 34 ºC.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

It was at this stage that we realized we had chosen a heating element of an unsuitable rating. Since it was rated at 100 W and 12 V, it required a current of ~8.3 A (which is pretty high) for optimal performance. However, this **hugely increased power demand** for our setup, and supplying the heating element with a much lower current of 1.5 A barely caused a temperature change. The heater was also a little too large for our culture vessel, causing air gaps in between the vessel and heater which reduced heating efficiency. We figured out that we would have to obtain a new heating element with lower power specifications, and proceeded to use a smaller 30 W heating element for the next iteration instead.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">5</span>Iteration 5: Improving temperature control</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

We replaced the single 100 W heating element with two smaller 30 W heating elements, to lower the power demand and to fit the culture vessel better.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We attached two 30 W heaters, each measuring 15 x 5 cm (shown in orange in the image), which covered the circumference of the culture vessel.

<div class="figure-grid single">
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/heaters-on-vessel.avif" alt="Render of the glass culture vessel with an orange band representing two 30 W heaters wrapped around its circumference"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 12. Two 30 W heaters cover the circumference of the vessel.</strong> The heaters are shown in orange.</figcaption></figure>
</div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

We were able to raise the temperature of water from 18 °C to 47 °C.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

The two 30 W heaters wrapped around the circumference of the vessel raised the water from 18 °C to 47 °C, well above the temperatures needed to culture *E. coli*, while drawing far less power than the 100 W element: both heaters together were supplied with just 1.5 A at 12 V (about 18 W in total). Covering the full circumference of the vessel gave even coverage, and the lower power draw made the system better suited to our energy-constrained design.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">6</span>Iteration 6: Fine-tuning temperature regulation</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

We initially used a PID controller, written as code on the Arduino, to regulate the heating elements so that the temperature would approach the setpoint smoothly.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We implemented the PID loop on the Arduino, using the DS18B20 <a class="cite" href="#ref-7">7</a> temperature reading as the input and the heater power (PWM duty cycle, 0-255) as the output. The gains were set to Kp = 30, Ki = 0.5, Kd = 10 and the loop was updated once per second. After running into problems with this approach, we replaced it with a simple on/off controller that switches the heaters on when the temperature is below the setpoint and off when it reaches it.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

The PID controller turned out to be overcomplicated for our setup, and delays in the response of the system arose. We then tested the much simpler on/off method, heating water from 18 °C to a setpoint of 36 °C. The water reached the setpoint in about 34 minutes and was then held there, as shown below. The detailed steady-state results are reported under [Experimental validation](#experimental-validation).

<div class="figure-grid single wide">
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/temp-regulation-36c.avif" alt="Line chart titled Bioreactor Temperature Profile (36 degrees C setpoint): temperature rises steadily from 18 degrees C to 36 degrees C over about 34 minutes, then holds flat at 36 degrees C for the rest of the 50-minute run"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 13. Simple on/off control held the temperature at the 36 °C setpoint.</strong></figcaption></figure>
</div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

Simple on/off control held the temperature within ±0.5 °C of the setpoint, which is more than precise enough for culturing *E. coli*. We realized we should have tested this much simpler method first: for a heated vessel, which responds slowly, PID control added complexity, tuning effort and delayed response without improving regulation. We therefore kept on/off control for the final design.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">7</span>Iteration 7: pH control</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

We added a pH sensor and acid and base dispensing, controlled by the Arduino microcontroller, to keep the culture at its pH setpoint.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We integrated a pH sensor (typically used for continuous monitoring of aquarium pH) to the vessel and calibrated its output with the Arduino voltage reading. We also integrated water pumps which were to be used to dispense HCl and NaOH solutions to adjust the pH accordingly.

<div class="figure-grid single">
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/prototype-ph-sensor.avif" alt="Bioreactor prototype with the pH sensor installed through the lid of the glass vessel, below a 3D-printed electronics tray held up by four supports"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 14. The pH sensor was integrated into the prototype.</strong> Bioreactor prototype with the pH sensor installed.</figcaption></figure>
</div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

We only tested the dispensing pumps using water, not acid or base solutions. The goal was to simulate the pH adjustment process and mainly to verify that the concept of acid/base dispensing for pH correction works.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

Calibrating the sensor output against the Arduino voltage reading allowed the Arduino to read the pH of the culture, and the pump test showed that the pumps can dispense liquid on command from the Arduino. Because we only tested the pumps with water, we have not yet verified that dosing HCl and NaOH brings the pH to its setpoint as intended. The next step is to test the dispensing with acid and base solutions, confirm that the pumps and tubing are compatible with them, and check how quickly and how closely the pH reaches the setpoint.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">8</span>Iteration 8: Sterility and vessel design</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

We chose a glass culture vessel that can be sterilized and made the non-autoclavable parts disposable or surface-sterilizable to ensure that the culture stays free of contamination.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We assembled the vessel with the 3D-printed lid consisting of six ports: four inlet ports for the dispensing of culture medium, acid/base for pH regulation, antifoam agent, and a gas inlet to supply oxygen to the broth; and two outlet ports, one for gas release to maintain internal gas pressure at ~1 atm, and another to transfer the final culture to the protein purification system. These ports were connected to sterilizable silicone tubing (ID 2.5 mm) via luer lock connectors.

Plastic luer lock connectors join the tubing to the lid ports.</strong></figcaption></figure>
</div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

We first ran the whole setup with water to check the electrical components, then ran the bioreactor with an *E. coli* culture for 10 hours. It regulated temperature reliably and supported growth on its own. We also tested the sterilization procedure by incubating sterile LB in the vessel overnight, and it showed no contamination. The full results are in [Experimental validation](#experimental-validation).

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

Testing with water first let us find electrical hazards before any culture was introduced. The vessel, six-port lid, tubing and connectors worked together as a complete, sterilizable system. Autoclaving sterilizes the glass vessel, and the parts that cannot be autoclaved are replaced or wiped with 70% ethanol.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">9</span>Iteration 9: Integration, controller and dashboard</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

We combined the sensors and actuators on an Arduino Mega and added a touchscreen dashboard where the user sets the temperature, pH and motor speed, all in a compact enclosure. We initially planned to use a 16x2 LCD screen for the bioreactor, but realized that handling a larger touchscreen LCD on the dashboard might be more convenient for astronauts. A touchscreen also lets us display more information.

<div class="figure-grid single">
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/lcd-welcome-screen.avif" alt="Touchscreen LCD showing the SHIHAB Bioreactor welcome screen with the text Touch screen to start, wired to an Arduino on a breadboard"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 18. A touchscreen runs the dashboard.</strong> The LCD showing the SHIHAB welcome screen.</figcaption></figure>
</div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We wired and programmed the controller, built the dashboard interface and housed everything in a 3D-printed enclosure consisting of four removable supports and a compartment at the top for the electronics. The electronics compartment also has a removable lid for convenient troubleshooting should it be necessary.

<div class="figure-grid single">
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/final-bioreactor.avif" alt="The assembled SHIHAB bioreactor: a 3D-printed base with the touchscreen and power switch, four supports holding the electronics tray at the top, and the glass vessel with the 3D-printed lid, rotor and heaters in the middle"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 19. </div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

We ran the full system for 10 hours again, for the final time, to check for any malfunctions. We found that everything worked as we planned. The sensors were responsive and managed to regulate the internal conditions of the culture chamber at the setpoint and no overheating or failure of components was observed. However, we did notice a small issue, which was that after a few hours, the LCD screen tended to go blank, even though the data was still being sent to the computer that we connected to the microcontroller for data acquisition (which was how we knew everything else was working as they should). After reading up on this issue, we suspected it could have been due to the higher current required to power the motors and heaters that was interfering with the signals being sent to the LCD.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

We were lucky to find that it was a simple fix that required separating the circuits of the high-current and low-current components to reduce their interference with each other. We did this for the final iteration of the bioreactor and managed to fix the issue of the LCD screen going blank.

This also showed us that a fault can appear only after several hours of running, so long full-system tests are necessary and short bench tests are not enough. Logging data to a computer in parallel with the display helped us tell a display fault apart from a system fault, and we would keep that logging in future versions.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter final">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">✓</span>Final bioreactor design</h4>

The SHIHAB bioreactor combines interchangeable glass vessels, a 3D-printed rotor and lid optimized for lower gravity, a stirring motor enclosed in an easily replaceable housing and coupled to the rotor so that it never touches the culture, two flexible heating elements, and temperature, pH and turbidity sensors on an Arduino Mega with a touchscreen dashboard. It measures 20 x 24 x 31 cm, weighs 2.2 kg and runs on a 30 W power supply.

</div>

### Protein purification system: filtration unit

<div class="iter-list">

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">1</span>Iteration 1: Passing culture through a fine filter</h4>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We initially tested filtering a sample mixture of food-grade *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* in distilled water (3.0 g yeast per 100 mL of water) through a fine filter (0.2 µm pore size) to perform an initial assessment of the feasibility of obtaining a clarified culture this way. The PVDF filter was fitted onto a syringe, which was repeatedly filled with the yeast mixture.

<div class="figure-grid" style="--fig-h: 200px; --fig-fit: cover;">
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/pvdf-filter.avif" alt="Two syringe filters: one in its blue-topped sterile packaging and one labelled Target2 PVDF 0.2 micrometre"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 20. A PVDF syringe filter was used for the first filtration test.</strong> The filter has a 0.2 µm pore size.</figcaption></figure>
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/pvdf-filter-syringe.avif" alt="A graduated syringe with the PVDF 0.2 micrometre filter fitted on its tip"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 21. The filter was fitted onto a syringe that was repeatedly filled with the yeast mixture.</strong></figcaption></figure>
</div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

As we expected, the filter was rapidly clogged even with a relatively small amount of culture being passed through it (~100 mL).

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

From this, we learned that a multi-stage filtration system was imperative to avoid clogging.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">2</span>Iteration 2</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

We then built a two-stage filtration system comprising two modules that were meant to be attached together, before being passed to the 0.2 µm PVDF filter. Our idea was to fit a filter material in between the modules and connect these modules together.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We 3D-printed two modules: an inlet module and an outlet module. The outlet module was designed to hold a filter disc, with a coarse filter (500 µm) in the first stage before being passed to the second stage of filtration (0.2 µm). The modules were joined with a simple press-fit connection and secured with four small silicone bands, then connected to the final 0.2 µm filter in Iteration 1.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

We passed 300 mL of *S. cerevisiae* culture through the system. The coarse first stage took up most of the cell mass, so the fine filter lasted longer than it did in Iteration 1. However, the space above the coarse filter filled with residue very quickly. Once that cake built up, flow slowed sharply and the first stage clogged. We also saw culture seeping out at the press-fit joints between modules.

<div class="figure-grid single">
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/filter-residue.avif" alt="Gloved hand holding a 3D-printed module with a filter disc covered in a pale layer of yeast residue"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 22. Yeast residue accumulated on the filter.</strong> Residue left on the filter after passing the culture through.</figcaption></figure>
</div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

Splitting filtration into stages helped, but each module needed much more room than we initially expected to hold residue before it started limiting flow. The press-fit connections were also not reliable enough to keep liquid contained.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">3</span>Iteration 3: Increasing residue capacity</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

To stop the residue from clogging the stages, we redesigned the modules with a much larger internal chamber above each filter. The idea was that solids could settle and collect there instead of packing directly onto the filter surface. We also added an additional middle filter module to increase the number of filtration stages and make the jump between stages much smaller. Now, the pore sizes of the three filtration stages are 500 µm, 11 µm and 0.2 µm.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We printed new modules with chamber heights of 20 mm (previously 4 mm) and a wider internal diameter of 42 mm (previously 38 mm). We also opted for FDM 3D-printing with PLA instead of SLA printing with resin for more rapid manufacturing of the filtration modules, given that we were still in the early stages of prototyping.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

The larger chambers greatly reduced clogging, and we could pass ~650 mL of culture before flow dropped noticeably. However, the system became bulky and heavy. It also held a large dead volume of liquid, which would mean losing product, and it was awkward to handle and assemble. The bigger press-fit joints also loosened more easily and still leaked.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

Bigger chambers solved clogging but conflicted with one of our core requirements: compactness. Our hardware has to be compact and simple enough for a space environment, where volume and mass are tightly limited. We needed to find the smallest module size that still held enough residue.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">4</span>Iteration 4: Balancing capacity and compactness</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

We designed several module variants with different chamber volumes to find the best trade-off between residue capacity and overall size.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We printed four versions with chamber heights of 5, 10, 15 and 20 mm, keeping all other dimensions constant.

<div class="figure-grid single">
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/module-sizes.avif" alt="Four 3D-printed filtration modules of increasing chamber height, shown side by side"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 23. We printed four module versions with chamber heights of 5, 10, 15 and 20 mm.</strong></figcaption></figure>
</div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

We ran the same volume of culture through each variant and recorded the volume filtered before flow dropped, along with the size and dead volume of each.

<div class="caption">

</div>

The 15 mm variant held enough residue to filter ~570 mL without clogging while staying ~25% smaller in chamber volume than the Iteration 3 design. Leaking at the joints, however, was still a problem, and the press-fit modules sometimes sat slightly misaligned, which tilted the filters.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

We settled on module dimensions that balanced residue capacity against compactness. The next priority was a secure, aligned connection between modules.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">5</span>Iteration 5: Threaded connections</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

We replaced the press-fit connections with screw threads, so the modules twist together. Threads give a tighter and more repeatable fit, keep the modules aligned, and let the filters be clamped evenly between modules.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We added threads (M42x3, class 6g, right-hand) to the ends of each module. Because tolerances matter for 3D-printed threads, we printed several test pairs to tune the clearance before printing full modules.

<div class="figure-grid single">
<figure><div class="photo-frame"><img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6341/wiki/content/hardware/threaded-modules.avif" alt="Three 3D-printed filtration modules side by side, two with screw threads on one end"></div><figcaption class="caption"><strong>Figure 24. Threaded connections join the filtration modules.</strong></figcaption></figure>
</div>

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

The threaded modules screwed together firmly, stayed aligned, and were quick to assemble and take apart. Leaking was much lower than with press-fit, but small leaks still appeared at the interfaces when the system was under pressure or after repeated use.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

Threads solved alignment and mechanical security, but plastic-on-plastic contact alone cannot form a fully liquid-tight seal. A dedicated sealing element was needed.

</div></div>
</div>

<div class="iter">
<h4 class="iter-title"><span class="iter-num">6</span>Iteration 6: Adding gaskets</h4>
<div class="iter-row design"><span class="iter-label">Design</span><div class="iter-body">

We added gaskets between each pair of modules so that tightening the threads compresses the gasket and seals the joint.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row build"><span class="iter-label">Build</span><div class="iter-body">

We laser-cut gaskets using a CO<sub>2</sub> laser from silicone sheets to fit the interface between modules and adjusted the module geometry to include a seat for each gasket.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row test"><span class="iter-label">Test</span><div class="iter-body">

We ran 500 mL of culture through the fully assembled system at a flow rate of 240 mL/min. No leaking was observed at any of the joints, and we verified that the gaskets could keep their seal over at least 30 assembly and use cycles.

</div></div>
<div class="iter-row learn"><span class="iter-label">Learn</span><div class="iter-body">

With threads and gaskets together, the system was secure, aligned and leak-proof. At this point, however, the number of filtration stages was fixed by the design. We realized that different cultures, volumes or purity targets could require more or fewer stages.

</div></div>
</div>

### Final design

Our final protein purification system is a modular, multi-stage filtration unit. It consists of an inlet module, an outlet module and any number of stackable middle modules, each housing a filter. Each module is sized to hold enough residue to avoid clogging while keeping the system as compact and simple as possible. Threaded connections keep the modules secure and aligned, and gaskets between them prevent leaking. Because the middle module is stackable, the number of filtration stages can be adjusted to match the culture and the level of clarification required. Any filter material of various pore sizes can be fitted into the filtration unit, as long as the diameter fits in the filtration module that is 42 mm in diameter, offering the user more flexibility in planning the most ideal set-up for protein purification.

## Experimental validation

### Computational fluid dynamics simulation

Before building the final rotor, we used computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to ask a question that is hard to test on the ground: how does the culture move when gravity is almost absent? On Earth, gravity does a lot of quiet work for a bioreactor. Heavier fluid sinks, lighter fluid and gas bubbles rise, and this natural convection helps mix the culture and carry oxygen through it <a class="cite" href="#ref-5">5</a>. In microgravity this help disappears, so the rotor has to do the mixing on its own. This is why the rotor configuration matters so much for SHIHAB.

Top row: vertical slice of the vessel with flow streamlines. Bottom row: view from above.

We simulated the same rotor at 200 rpm under the gravity levels of Earth, Mars, the Moon and transit. The results show that the flow pattern depends strongly on gravity. In transit and on the Moon, we saw regions of the vessel where circulation stayed confined to one part of the vessel instead of spreading through the entire culture. Fluid in the other regions moved much more slowly and was mixed far less.

This matters because oxygen reaches the cells only as fast as the culture carries it. Where circulation is limited, oxygen is distributed unevenly: some cells receive plenty while others are starved, and since oxygen availability affects how much protein the bacteria produce, **uneven mixing directly limits our yield**. The simulation therefore tells us that a rotor that works well on Earth cannot simply be assumed to work in space, and that the rotor design has to be chosen and tested for microgravity conditions so that circulation reaches the whole vessel.

### Sterility and vessel validation

We first tested the whole setup with water to identify any potential hazards with the electrical components. After verifying that the bioreactor could run independently and perform accurate and reliable temperature regulation for 10 hours, we tested the bioreactor with an *E. coli* culture for 10 hours. We observed that the bioreactor was able to support the growth of *E. coli* independently as well and performed our sterilization procedure.

To check that the procedure works, the glass vessel was soaked in bleach for 30 min, emptied, thoroughly washed and rinsed with deionized/distilled water. It was then autoclaved at 121 °C and approximately 15 psi for at least two hours (glassware cycle, following the laboratory autoclave SOP), filled with sterile LB medium and autoclaved again. The LB-filled vessel was then placed in a shaking incubator at 37 °C overnight to test for microbial contamination. Afterwards, the LB showed no visible turbidity and its OD<sub>600</sub> was approximately 0.001, indicating no detectable growth. For comparison, an overnight *E. coli* culture grown under the same conditions reached an OD<sub>600</sub> of ~1.36, which is what we would expect if the vessel had not been sterile.

<p class="callout-label">What we learned</p>

Testing with water first let us find electrical hazards before any culture was introduced. The full setup then ran independently for 10 hours with reliable temperature regulation and supported *E. coli* growth, which showed that the vessel, six-port lid, sensors, rotor, tubing and sterilizable connectors work together as a complete system. ## Hardware, sensing, and sterility

<div class="popup-grid">

<div class="popup-card">
<button type="button" class="popup-open"><span class="popup-icon"><svg viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="1.6" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" aria-hidden="true"><path d="M12 3l7 3v5c0 4.5-3 8-7 10-4-2-7-5.5-7-10V6l7-3z"/><path d="M9 12l2 2 4-4"/></svg></span><span class="popup-face"><span class="popup-title">Sterilization</span><span class="popup-desc">Keeping the culture and purification path free of contamination throughout the run.</span><span class="popup-hint">Tap to read more</span></span></button>
<div class="popup-more">

**Glass culture vessel:** The glass vessel was first soaked in bleach for 30 min with the culture, then emptied and thoroughly washed to remove residual culture material. It was then rinsed with deionized water, filled with LB medium and steam-sterilized in an autoclave at 121 °C and approximately 15 psi for two hours (covered in aluminum foil), using the appropriate cycle for glassware and in accordance with the laboratory’s validated autoclave SOP. Culture-contacting tubing and connectors are also autoclaved. After completion of the cycle and return of the chamber to safe pressure and temperature, the vessel was allowed to cool and dry before being handled. Steam sterilization at 121 °C is an established method for sterilizing laboratory glassware.

**Non-autoclavable and disposable components:** Currently, the 3D-printed rotor and vessel lid in the prototype are disposed of after experiments as they are made of non-autoclavable PLA. In future iterations of the prototype, they will be replaced with stainless steel to be compatible with autoclaving.

**Remaining bioreactor surfaces:** Non-autoclavable external surfaces and reusable components that do not directly contact the culture were first cleaned of visible residue and then wiped thoroughly with 70% ethanol, ensuring complete coverage and allowing the surfaces to air-dry.

</div>
</div>

<div class="popup-card">
<button type="button" class="popup-open"><span class="popup-icon"><svg viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="1.6" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" aria-hidden="true"><path d="M12 3l8 4.5v9L12 21l-8-4.5v-9L12 3z"/><path d="M12 12l8-4.5M12 12v9M12 12L4 7.5"/></svg></span><span class="popup-face"><span class="popup-title">Materials</span><span class="popup-desc">Components selected to meet NASA material guidelines for spaceflight hardware.</span><span class="popup-hint">Tap to read more</span></span></button>
<div class="popup-more">

While developing the SHIHAB platform, we interviewed Saudi astronaut Rayyanah Barnawi, who advised us to build our platform in accordance with NASA guidelines. We took this advice on board by using NASA-STD-6016B, Standard Materials and Processes Requirements for Spacecraft, as our reference when choosing and reviewing the materials in the bioreactor. We also took into consideration the density of materials chosen, opting for more lightweight options whenever possible as reducing payload mass was a priority for our project. <a class="cite" href="#ref-8">8</a>

**Glass culture vessel:** Inorganic glass is exempt from the standard’s flammability and toxic offgassing requirements, and it withstands autoclaving, making it a suitable choice for the part that holds the culture.

**3D-printed components:** The standard applies its flammability, toxic offgassing and vacuum outgassing requirements to 3D-printed nonmetallic hardware just as it does to any other nonmetallic material. For this reason, the PLA rotor and vessel lid are limited to the prototype and will be replaced with stainless steel in future iterations.

**Stainless steel:** For corrosion-resistant steels, the standard notes that stabilized or low-carbon grades such as 316L and 304L do not need post-weld heat treatment, and it prohibits free-machining alloys such as 303 in mission-critical hardware that may be exposed to moisture. Because our culture is aqueous, these considerations will guide our choice of grade.

**Replaceable parts:** The standard requires materials to last the useful life of the hardware or be identified as limited-life items that can be maintained. Our modular design lets the tubing, connectors, sensors, pumps and motors be taken apart and replaced individually, rather than the whole platform being limited by its shortest-lived part.

This reflects design-stage alignment with the standard and is not a formal NASA qualification.

</div>
</div>

<div class="popup-card">
<button type="button" class="popup-open"><span class="popup-icon"><svg viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="1.6" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" aria-hidden="true"><rect x="7" y="7" width="10" height="10" rx="1.5"/><path d="M10 3v4M14 3v4M10 17v4M14 17v4M3 10h4M3 14h4M17 10h4M17 14h4"/></svg></span><span class="popup-face"><span class="popup-title">Sensors and actuators</span><span class="popup-desc">Sensors, an Arduino Mega and actuators work together to keep culture conditions stable.</span><span class="popup-hint">Tap to read more</span></span></button>
<div class="popup-more">

Sensors monitor the culture, an Arduino Mega decides what needs to change, and actuators carry it out. Together they close the feedback loops that keep conditions stable without the user having to check on the culture.

#### Sensors

**Temperature sensor:** A DS18B20 digital temperature sensor measures the temperature of the culture. The Arduino compares it with the user’s setpoint and switches the heating elements on and off to hold the culture at the target temperature. <a class="cite" href="#ref-7">7</a>

**pH sensor:** Measures the pH of the culture. As the bacteria grow, the pH of the medium drifts, so the Arduino uses this reading to trigger acid or base dispensing and bring it back to the setpoint.

**Turbidity sensor:** An Adafruit turbidity sensor measures how cloudy the culture is, which tracks the density of the growing bacteria (optical density). This lets the bioreactor follow growth in real time and trigger induction automatically once the optimal density is reached.

#### Controller

**Arduino Mega:** All three sensors are connected to an Arduino Mega, which runs the temperature and pH control loops and commands the actuators. The current temperature and pH are shown to the user on the touchscreen dashboard.

#### Actuators

**Heating elements:** Two 30 W, 12 V flexible heating elements hold the culture at the temperature setpoint, switched on and off by the Arduino based on the temperature sensor reading.

**Acid/base dispensing:** Small volumes of acid or base are added to the culture to keep the pH at the setpoint, triggered by the pH sensor reading. The water pumps used for this are rated at 5 V.

**Stirring motor:** Turns the rotor to mix the culture, which supports even oxygen and temperature distribution. The rotor speed is shown on the dashboard as Motor RPM.

**Syringe pumps with custom linear actuators:** The protein purification system uses syringe pumps to dispense precise volumes of each buffer, using 60 mL syringes. We built the linear actuator for these pumps ourselves from stepper motors and rails: the 12 V stepper motor moves in fixed steps, so the number of steps sets how far the syringe plunger travels and therefore how much liquid is dispensed, while the rails keep the motion in a straight line. Each pump is pre-calibrated for accurate dispensing.

## Beyond space

The same features that make SHIHAB suited to space (a compact, lightweight and low-power design, closed fluid paths and modular, replaceable parts) also make it a good fit for places on Earth that are hard to reach. In remote villages, disaster zones, offshore sites and other communities far from a hospital or a pharmacy, getting medicine can take days or weeks, and many therapeutics do not keep well on the way. A platform that fits in a suitcase and runs on a small power supply could instead produce what is needed on demand, close to the people who need it, from a small, stable supply of ingredients.
