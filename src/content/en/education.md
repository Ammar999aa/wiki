## Overview

Synthetic biology and genetic engineering are transforming agriculture, healthcare and manufacturing. Yet in Saudi Arabia and the wider Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, synthetic biology is still a new and unfamiliar discipline, especially in schools. Secondary school curricula focus on general biology, leaving a gap in synthetic biology, bioengineering and biotechnology, and most learning resources in the field exist only in English.

At KAUST iGEM, our vision goes beyond the bench. We believe public literacy and educational empowerment are essential to scientific innovation. As researchers at King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST), we are in a unique position to introduce these topics to our local community and to the next generation of Saudi scientists and innovators.

## Copy Paste Plants

### Overview

On Tuesday, April 7, 2026, KAUST iGEM ran Copy Paste Plants as part of the Sustainability Family Food Night at The KAUST School (TKS), organized by the Center of Excellence for Sustainable Food Security and TKS. The evening brought together students, families, community members and experts to explore plants, sustainable food, innovative technology and environmental science.

### The Activity

Our booth ran **Copy Paste Plants**, a hands-on plant cloning activity that introduced children to the idea of genetic identity. Children learned to propagate a pothos plant from a cutting in three steps: get your plant, make the cut, and watch it grow. Because the new plant grows from the parent's own tissue, it is an exact genetic copy, which gave us a simple, tangible way to explain genetic identity and similarity to elementary-aged learners.

## Kallpa Multiplier Mentorship (Peru)

### Context

The use of genetically modified organisms (GMOs) has been debated for decades. Latin America is home to a significant share of the world's transgenic crops, with Brazil and Paraguay standing out. Peru, however, has taken a stance of strong opposition to protect its agrobiodiversity and organic gastronomy. A moratorium under Law No. 31.111 legally restricts the entry, cultivation and production of Living Modified Organisms (LMOs).

In April 2026, Peru approved Ministerial Resolution No. D000068-2026-MINAM-DM. It states that a plant edited with CRISPR to modify or silence one of its own genes, without introducing foreign DNA, is not classified as an LMO. Such plants would be exempt from the moratorium and could potentially be grown commercially.

### What We Identified

Despite this significant change, farmers, students and the public reacted with concern, fear and confusion, often shaped by misinformation about the safety, environmental impact and consequences of GMOs and gene editing. To understand the situation, we partnered with Kallpa Greenz.

After several interviews with the project leader, we identified three gaps:

<div class="card-grid row">

<div>

#### Knowledge Gap

Many farmers in rural areas lack access to the scientific concepts behind the technologies being debated.

</div>

<div>

#### Access Gap

Reliable scientific information is unequally available, because few educational resources are tailored to rural farming contexts.

</div>

<div>

#### Model Gap

There is no sustainable, replicable educational model that can be rolled out to new communities and new generations of mentors.

</div>

</div>

### Our Educational Strategy: A Multiplier Model

We chose a train-the-trainer, or multiplier, model: KAUST iGEM trains Kallpa's youth volunteers, who then run weekly in-person sessions with rural farmers. Our goal was not to push volunteers toward a particular stance on GMOs, but to give them the knowledge and tools to understand what GMOs are, how they work, how they are used, and how they are regulated. We organized the course into four connected modules.

## Tawasol Hackathon

### Overview

<blockquote>

Once a powerful new biotechnology exists, who decides how it is used safely?

</blockquote>

The Tawasol Hackathon, held August 28–30, 2026 and co-organized with NYU Abu Dhabi iGEM, asked high school (grades 10–12) and undergraduate students a question most students never get to consider. Teams focused on the governance of SHIHAB, our platform for in-situ biomanufacturing of medicines for space missions and remote settings on Earth.

The hackathon began as the SHIHAB Horizon Hackathon and was renamed Tawasol (تواصل, "connection") when NYU Abu Dhabi iGEM joined as co-organizer. It became the first project of the Tawasol Initiative, our coalition of MENA iGEM teams, described on our <a href="/science-communication#tawasol-uniting-mena-igem-teams">Science Communication page</a>.

### Structure

The hackathon ran as a virtual pitch sprint followed by a hybrid Grand Finale in the KAUST B20 lobby. Teams chose one of three tracks:

<div class="card-grid">

<div>

#### Track A — Safety and Containment

How to keep engineered organisms safe in closed or extreme environments.

</div>

<div>

#### Track B — Public Trust and Ethics

How to earn public trust in biomanufactured medicines.

</div>

<div>

#### Track C — Integration and Liability

Who is responsible when things go wrong.

</div>

</div>

Each team produced a formal policy whitepaper, judged by external experts. The Grand Finale featured the deans of KAUST's BESE and BioMed divisions.
