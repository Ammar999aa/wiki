Reading stress tolerance out of the Empty Quarter

The world's largest sand desert holds microbes that survive UV, desiccation, thermal swing and anoxia daily. We built a five-layer screening pipeline that reads their metagenomes and returns a ranked, QC-flagged candidate set for synthesis. Then we spent a second campaign trying to break it, and it broke in four places. This page documents the pipeline, the audit, and what survived.

01

What this is, and what it is not

Scope, stated up front

This page describes a screening pipeline and its audit. It is not a mechanistic model of a biological system, and the ranking function at its centre is a heuristic: six literature thresholds combined under hand-set weights, with no fitted parameters and no validation against measured expression or solubility outcomes. We say this here rather than letting a reader infer it in §06.

Two pieces of genuine statistical modelling do sit inside it, and they are the parts we would defend: the depth-matched resampling null that overturned our own conservation claim (§04), and the Dirichlet weight-sensitivity analysis that showed one of our four category rankings carries no information at all (§07). Both are models of our own method's reliability rather than of biology, and that is the honest description of them.

A metagenome is not a parts list. It is twenty thousand plausible open reading frames, most of which will never fold, never express, or never purify. The engineering question is narrow and unforgiving:

Design question

Given only sequence data from an environment we cannot culture, which proteins are worth the cost of a synthesis order and a wet-lab slot?

Answering it by homology alone fails, because a BLAST hit tells you what a sequence resembles, not whether the molecule behind it is stable, small, chargeable, folded, or even complete. So the screen scores each candidate along five independent axes, each grounded in a different kind of evidence, and only then combines them.

Layer 1 · Identity
Annotation

Bakta gene calls cross-checked against independent eggNOG orthology. Disagreement is treated as disqualifying, not as noise.

Layer 2 · Behaviour
Physicochemistry

Instability index, molecular weight, isoelectric point, GRAVY and aliphatic index: the properties that decide whether purification is cheap or impossible.

Layer 3 · History
Conservation

Shannon entropy over depth-matched alignments, so that "conserved" means something other than "we aligned a lot of sequences."

Layer 4 · Form
Structure

ESMFold CA-pLDDT, calibrated against three solved crystal structures spanning three distinct fold families.

Layer 5 · Provenance
Source quality

GTDB-Tk taxonomy plus CheckM completeness of the bin the gene came from. A perfect gene in a 33%-complete bin is not a finding.

Adversarial
Rigor review

Twelve audits run against our own output. Eight candidates and two headline claims did not survive; see §07.

03

Physicochemical layer: will it survive a bench?

Every Tier-1 sequence goes through the ProtParam module of Biopython [6]. Four of its outputs feed the composite score. Two others, GRAVY and the aliphatic index, are computed but deliberately left out of the score; we use them as consistency checks, for reasons the expandable note at the end of this section explains.

How the instability index is computed

Guruprasad, Reddy and Pandit [14] observed that certain dipeptides occur at measurably different frequencies in proteins with short in vivo half-lives than in stable ones. They assigned each of the 400 possible dipeptides a weight from that frequency difference. The index slides a two-residue window along the sequence, sums the weights, and normalises by length:

II = 10 L Σi=1 WDIPEP(xixi+1)

L = sequence length · WDIPEP = Guruprasad dipeptide instability weight

The original study put the cutoff at 40: below it, a protein was predicted stable in E. coli. Two limits are worth stating plainly. The weights were fitted on a small set of proteins whose half-lives were known in 1990, and the index knows nothing about structure, so it cannot see a stabilising disulfide or a destabilising exposed loop. It is a cheap prior, not a measurement, which is why we cross-check it against the fold predictions in §05 rather than trusting it alone.

Why isoelectric point is worth 15% of the score

The reason is economic. Biopython computes pI by bisection on the Henderson–Hasselbalch charge sum, finding the pH at which the summed charge of the ionisable side chains and termini crosses zero. A protein whose pI sits below 5 or above 9 is far from neutral pH at working conditions, so it binds an ion-exchange column cleanly and can be captured without an affinity tag. No His-tag means no protease step and no leftover tag distorting the downstream assay. For a team with one summer and a shared FPLC, that difference decides whether a candidate gets characterised at all.

Why desert proteins are not thermophilic proteins

A reasonable prior is that desert organisms should yield thermostable proteins. Our data says otherwise, and the disagreement is informative. Thermophilic proteomes are marked by a high aliphatic index, meaning a large volume fraction of Ala, Val, Ile and Leu packing a rigid hydrophobic core. Our candidates' aliphatic indices sit in the mesophilic range.

The ecology explains it. Sand at depth buffers temperature far more than surface air does; what the resident organisms actually face is water stress and UV, not sustained heat. The proteome we recovered is a desiccation-and-radiation proteome: trehalose and ectoine biosynthesis, photolyases, cold-shock RNA chaperones. We report this as a negative result rather than suppressing it. Two caveats belong with it. The aliphatic index is not one of the six scored dimensions and is not in the candidate table below, so this observation is currently an assertion rather than a figure you can check; putting the per-candidate values on the page is on our list. And the comparison has no matched control proteome, so "mesophilic range" means "against published reference ranges", not against a proteome we processed the same way.

A check that cannot fail

An earlier version of this page offered a consistency argument here: that no candidate shows the contradictory signature of high pLDDT with a poor instability index, and that this supports the physicochemical predictions. That argument is circular and we have withdrawn it. Instability below 40 is an upstream filter, so every surviving candidate is under 40 by construction (the maximum in the final set is 39.65). The check had no way to fail. It is also why the dashed "instability 40" guide in Figure 2 has no data on its right-hand side, and why the stability dimension in §06 partly double-counts a threshold that was already applied before scoring.

04

Conservation layer: and the claim it cost us

Conservation is the one axis that says something about biology rather than about handling. If a position has stayed the same across hundreds of divergent desert organisms, selection has been holding it there, and mutating it is likely to cost function.

How the statistic is built

Three steps, each with a reason. First, CD-HIT [4] collapses the family down to representatives at 90% identity. Its greedy incremental algorithm sorts sequences longest-first, makes the longest a cluster seed, and assigns each subsequent sequence to the first seed it matches above threshold, using short-word counting to skip most alignments outright. This step removes 73–89% of the sequences in our families, and skipping it would be fatal: metagenomes oversample abundant organisms, so an unfiltered alignment measures who was abundant in the sand, not what selection preserved.

Second, MAFFT [5] aligns the representatives. Third, we take each alignment column c, read off the frequency pa,c of each amino acid a in it, and compute Shannon entropy [17]. A column where every sequence agrees has entropy 0; a column drawing uniformly from all twenty residues approaches log₂20 ≈ 4.32 bits. We call a column conserved when its entropy falls below τ = 0.5 bits, and report the family statistic as the fraction of columns that qualify.

H(c) = − Σa∈A pa,c log2 pa,c · Cons = |{ c : H(c) < τ }| C

A = 20 amino acids · C = alignment columns · τ = entropy threshold

Claim retracted

Version 1 of this project reported ibpAB as the most conserved stress protein family at 56–61%. That claim does not survive its own null model and has been withdrawn.

The failure is a statistical artefact, and any entropy-over-alignment pipeline is exposed to it. Column entropy depends on how many sequences went into the column. With 64 sequences you have 64 chances to observe a substitution; with 226 you have 226. Real diversity that exists in both families will surface in the deep alignment and stay hidden in the shallow one, so the shallow family scores as more conserved for no biological reason at all. Our alignment depths range from 7 to 230 sequences, roughly thirty-fold, which is more than enough to swamp the signal we were trying to measure.

The fix is to compare families at equal depth. We subsample every family without replacement down to a common N (18 sequences for the enrichment set, 20 for the isolates, each set by the smallest usable family), recompute the statistic, and repeat 2,000 times to get a distribution rather than a point estimate. Sampling without replacement matters here: drawing duplicates would insert artificial agreement into a column and deflate its entropy.

At matched depth the result inverts. csp, not ibpAB, is the most conserved family, in both datasets independently. We report the corrected numbers and leave the retracted ones on the page.

Why the permutation test could not settle this

Our first null model shuffles residues within alignment columns, preserving each column's amino-acid composition while destroying cross-column structure, and recomputes the statistic over 1,000 permutations. Every family clears it at p < 0.001, and 33 of the 37 testable rows saturate at the resolution floor with the null standard deviation rounding to zero.

That is not the vindication it looks like. With twenty or more real, non-identical protein sequences, producing a near-unanimous column by chance under full row scrambling is combinatorially near-impossible, so every real alignment clears the bar regardless of how constrained the family actually is. The row-shuffle null is a valid existence test — "this signal is not literal noise" — and has no resolving power for ranking families against each other, which is exactly what the retracted claim needed. The matched-depth subsampling in Figure 3 is the test that does have that power, and it is the one that overturned the result.

One v1 conservation claim passed both tests unaltered, and it is the negative one: relBE really is the least conserved family we examined, last of nineteen in both datasets independently, though by a modest 2–10 point margin rather than a dramatic one.

05

Structure layer: calibrating what pLDDT means here

All 45 candidates were folded with ESMFold [7], 79 structures across both versions of the project, on the KAUST Ibex cluster.

Why a language model instead of AlphaFold

AlphaFold2 [8] builds its prediction from a multiple sequence alignment assembled by searching large sequence databases for homologs. ESMFold replaces that search with a protein language model, ESM-2, trained on unlabelled sequence; the evolutionary information AlphaFold reads out of an MSA is instead held in the model's internal representations, and a folding head reads structure directly from those. Lin and colleagues report accuracy somewhat below AlphaFold2 on well-covered proteins, in exchange for an order-of-magnitude speedup from dropping the search.

That tradeoff suits our data. Many of these sequences come from uncultured desert organisms with few close relatives in public databases, so the MSA an AlphaFold run could assemble would be thin, and a thin MSA is where AlphaFold degrades. Running MSA-free removes a failure mode we would otherwise have had to characterise per candidate.

What the confidence number is

pLDDT is the model's predicted score on the lDDT-Cα metric [9], which compares local interatomic distances between a model and a reference without superposing them. The network emits it per residue, on a 0–100 scale, and both AlphaFold and ESMFold write it into the B-factor column of the output PDB. We read the mean over Cα atoms.

Method disambiguation

Two pLDDT numbers exist for each of our proteins and they differ by roughly 13 points. The raw prediction tensor averages over all 37 atom slots per residue, most of which are padding for atoms a given residue does not have, which drags the mean down. The Cα B-factor mean does not. Every number on this page uses the Cα method, which is also the convention AlphaFold established [8]. We mention this because both numbers exist in our output files, and a reader comparing them without knowing which is which would find a contradiction that is not there.

Calibration against solved structures

A confidence metric is only useful once you know what it predicts, and pLDDT bands published for AlphaFold do not automatically transfer to a different architecture on a different sequence distribution. So we ran the production pipeline, the same inference script and the same parser, on proteins whose structures have been solved, and measured Cα RMSD against the deposited coordinates. The three were chosen to span the folds our candidates actually occupy rather than to be easy.

The model marked as uncertain precisely the region that does not exist in the real protein. We take that as grounds for reading a low-pLDDT terminus as a disorder signal rather than as a failed prediction, and it is why the flexible-region flags in our per-candidate output are worth looking at.

CspA is also the closest thing we have to a positive control for the flagship candidate: the desert cspC shares its fold and 56.7% sequence identity, and scores 90.3 against CspA's 88.6.

06

The scoring rule: how we chose it, and what we locked

This section decides one thing and then stops deciding it. We compare candidate scoring rules against measured data once, here, and commit to a single rule. Everything after this section uses that rule and no other.

The six dimensions

Each dimension maps a raw measurement onto the interval [0, 1] through a step function, and the six sub-scores combine under a fixed weight vector.

The step functions are not laziness. Each threshold is a published decision boundary: instability 40 from Guruprasad [14], pLDDT 80 from the AlphaFold confidence bands [8] as re-checked against our own benchmark, and the tag-free pI windows from ion-exchange practice. A smooth interpolation between those points would imply we know the shape of the function between them, and we do not.

Testing the weights against measured data

Hand-set weights are an assumption. We tested them without waiting for our own wet lab, by running the scoring rule over eSOL [31], which reports measured solubility for most of the E. coli proteome. Structure confidence came from AlphaFold DB [8] rather than our own ESMFold run, which the three benchmarks in §05 show agree to within 3 pLDDT points. The test covers the four dimensions carrying 80% of the weight, on 2,262 cytoplasmic proteins.

The remaining 20% stays untested, and the reason is worth stating rather than hiding. Conservation and annotation do not exist to predict solubility; they exist to test whether the gene is real. A candidate can be perfectly soluble and still be a misannotated fragment, which is exactly the failure §07 caught four times. So no solubility dataset can validate those two dimensions — not eSOL, not a larger one. It is a mismatch of objective, not a gap we could have closed with more data. Testing them properly needs a set of genes with known-correct and known-wrong annotations, which we do not have.

What the test found

The score works, and it is beaten by the simplest possible alternative. Two causes, both measured. The structure dimension carries 25% of the weight and is close to constant: 94.5% of these proteins clear pLDDT 80 and score 1.0, so the dimension separates almost nothing, and on its own it is slightly worse than chance. The one dimension that does predict solubility, size, carries only 15%. The rule dilutes its own best signal.

Why we did not refit the weights

The obvious response is to reweight toward size. We tested that and rejected it, for a reason specific to metagenomic data.

In eSOL every protein is a complete, real protein, so "small" means a small protein. In a metagenome assembly "small" often means a gene broken by a contig break. Across our candidates, molecular weight correlates with ORF completeness at ρ = +0.144: the smaller a candidate, the likelier it is a fragment. Raising the size weight to 50% promotes a 6.0 kDa uvrB at 7% ORF completeness and a 12.2 kDa mutS at 12% into the top eight. A rule tuned on complete proteins rewards broken ones here.

The second reason is that the score is not only a solubility predictor. Purifiability is in it for an economic reason, tag-free capture, which eSOL cannot evaluate at all; conservation and annotation are there to test whether the gene is real. Refitting everything to predict solubility would optimise a different objective from the one the rule exists to serve.

The locked rule

So the fix is not new weights. It is two gates applied before scoring, each derived from a failure the audits in §07 found, and each categorical rather than graded because the failures they catch are categorical.

What the locked rule changes, and what it does not

cspC remains the top candidate. We re-ranked all 45 candidates under four different weightings, including the size-heavy one eSOL favours, and cspC is first under every one of them. The flagship does not depend on the weight choice.

The desiccation degeneracy survives every rule. Those eight candidates have identical sub-scores on every dimension, so no reweighting and no gate separates them. They remain an unranked shortlist, and that is a property of the candidates rather than of the scoring.

Stability and structure together still carry half the weight. That is a deliberate choice with a consequence: the score is optimised for expressibility, not for biological novelty. A fascinating but aggregation-prone protein will rank below a boring, well-behaved one. That is correct for a rule whose output is a synthesis order, but it means the score should never be read as a measure of scientific interest.

07

Breaking our own pipeline on purpose

Every threshold in the previous section was set by hand. That makes the ranking an assumption, and an assumption we had never tested. In July 2026 we ran twelve audits against our own candidate set, with the aim of finding out which of our results were artefacts. Several were.

The audits ran in two rounds: seven adversarial checks, then five follow-ups closing the data gaps the first round exposed. By the end, 53 candidates had become 45, two headline claims were retracted, and one round-one verdict was itself overturned. What follows is the four tests that changed something; the full register is in the expandable table at the end of the section.

Test 1: does the ranking survive its own weights?

The question is whether the top candidate is a property of the data or a property of our weight choices. We attacked it two ways. First, one-at-a-time: perturb each of the six weights by ±10, ±20 and ±50%, rescale the remaining five to keep the vector summing to one, and re-rank. That is 36 runs per category, and it tells you which single dimension the ranking is most sensitive to.

One-at-a-time perturbation misses interactions, though, so the second test samples the whole weight space at once. We draw 1,000 weight vectors from a Dirichlet distribution centred on the baseline. The Dirichlet is the natural choice because every draw from it is already a valid weight vector, non-negative and summing to one, so no rescaling step is needed and no corner of the simplex is unreachable. Its concentration parameter sets how far the draws stray from the baseline. For each draw we re-rank and record whether the original winner is still winning; the fraction that says yes is the P(Top-1) figure below.

Consequence

The eight desiccation candidates are now presented as an undifferentiated shortlist rather than a ranked list. Anaerobic Respiration's top pick is reported as "narI or the frd cluster", since it wins by 0.015 points and flips under four of the thirty-six single-weight perturbations.

Test 2: are the genes actually complete?

Metagenome assembly breaks contigs wherever coverage runs out, and a gene caller will annotate whatever fragment sits on the broken contig with a confident family name. Nothing in the composite score notices. A 172-residue fragment of a 353-residue protein can score well on every dimension we measure, because a fragment of a stable protein is still stable-looking, and ESMFold will fold it confidently.

So we added a dimension the score does not use but a reviewer would ask for: ORF completeness, the candidate's length as a percentage of the canonical full-length ortholog for its family, taken from E. coli K-12 where a reference exists. The result reframed an entire category.

Test 3: does an independent method agree on gene identity?

Our two annotation tools reach a gene name by different routes. Bakta [1] assigns names by alignment-free matching against a curated protein database, optimised for speed and standardisation across a whole genome. eggNOG-mapper [2] places each sequence into a precomputed orthologous group in the eggNOG hierarchy [3] and takes the name from the group's consensus. Because the routes are independent, a disagreement between them is informative.

Our rule was to treat any disagreement as disqualifying rather than to adjudicate it. We are not in a position to decide which of two mature annotation tools is right about a sequence from an organism nobody has cultured, and a candidate whose identity is contested is not one to spend a synthesis order on. Four candidates went out this way, one of them only in round two. Where the question was about a catalytic domain rather than a gene name, we used InterProScan [12], which scans against the member databases of InterPro and reports domain hits with their boundaries.

The headline that broke

Version 1 of this project named DUF3494 as a novel bacterial ice-binding domain, and it was the most quotable thing we had. It is gone. The sequence runs 1,349 aa against a family norm of 150–300, has no independent eggNOG support at any evidence tier, and reads as a bacterial surface-adhesion repeat protein that happens to contain one DUF3494 domain hit. Calling that an antifreeze protein would have needed evidence we never had.

Test 4: how good is the bin the gene came from?

This is the audit we should have run first. Most of these genes come from metagenome-assembled genomes, and a gene inherits the reliability of the bin it was called in. CheckM [11] estimates that reliability by looking for a set of marker genes expected to be present in single copy for the bin's lineage: the fraction found gives completeness, and markers found more than once give contamination. Extracting those numbers exposed the highest-risk candidate in the project, which was, at the time, our recommended DNA-repair pick.

Top pick withdrawn

OGEHGG_01321 (recA, composite 0.85) carries three independent problems at once. The ORF is 49% of full length. Bakta flags it as a probable pseudogene. And its source bin is 33.7% CheckM-complete, below even the 50% floor the MIMAG standard sets for calling a metagenome-assembled genome a low-quality draft [13]. The runner-up sits in a 48.6%-complete bin. Neither is usable as a headline result.

Replacement: phrB (MJNEPN_02265 and three siblings). All four are 93–103% ORF-complete with clean bin quality, carry EC 4.1.99.3 as strong functional evidence, and fold at CA-pLDDT 88–92. The composite score is lower and the chance of it working is considerably higher. This is not a hand-picked exception: Gate 1 removes every truncated recA, mutS and uvr call on ORF completeness alone, and the four phrB candidates are simply what is left of the category once the rule runs.

Test 5: what is the screen structurally unable to see?

The four tests above all ask whether a candidate is what we said it is. This one asks a different question: is there a property that decides whether a protein can be made at all, that our six dimensions have no way of representing? There is, and it disqualifies most of a category.

Blind spot found 2026-08-26 — integral membrane proteins

Seven of the eight Anaerobic Respiration candidates are polytopic integral membrane proteins. narI is the membrane-embedded di-heme cytochrome b subunit of respiratory nitrate reductase; frdC and frdD are the membrane anchor subunits of fumarate reductase. Their GRAVY values run from +0.49 to +1.04, while every one of the other 37 candidates in the set is negative. Only narJ, a cytoplasmic molybdenum-cofactor assembly chaperone, is soluble.

The screen scored them 0.71–0.81, which ranks them above every phrB (0.515–0.62) and both ibpA candidates (0.605). In other words, after cspC the highest-scoring things this screen offers a wet lab are proteins that will not fold in a standard cytoplasmic expression host. GRAVY is computed for every candidate and then deliberately excluded from the score (§03), so hydrophobicity carries exactly zero weight. Nothing else in the six dimensions correlates with membrane insertion: a membrane anchor is small, stable, confidently folded and well annotated, so it scores well on four dimensions at once.

Consequence. This finding is what Gate 2 of the locked rule implements. Seven of the eight Anaerobic Respiration candidates do not pass it, so the category contributes one construct rather than eight. The sequences and annotations are real and stay in the published set; what changes is that they are no longer offered as targets for a first expression campaign. The gate is categorical rather than a seventh weighted dimension because the failure it catches is categorical, and §10 keeps it as a pre-registered prediction to be tested rather than a correction to be assumed.

What the screen still cannot tell you

Four gaps remain open. Each needs new computation or new wet-lab work rather than better bookkeeping, so none of them will close on their own. We put them here rather than in a footnote because a reviewer will find them anyway.

Gap 1 · Taxonomy
Part of the assembly set never ran GTDB-Tk

Several long-read assembly batches carry Kraken2/NCBI taxonomy only. Fixing two mapping errors raised GTDB coverage from 49% to 61.3%, but the remaining batches need a fresh GTDB-Tk run before any phylogenetic-breadth claim can be said to cover them.

Gap 2 · Bin quality
14 of the 45 have no CheckM data

Largely the same batches. Among the 31 that do have it, 10 fall below 90% completeness and 1 exceeds 5% contamination. Counted across all 53 candidates ever considered, including the eight later removed, the figures are 17 without data, 11 below 90% and 2 over 5%. We can flag what we measured; we cannot vouch for what we did not.

Gap 3 · Provenance
92% come from MAGs, not isolates

No physical strain exists for most candidates, and 21% rest on a single genome's evidence. OGEHGG_01321 specifically needs re-assembly or PCR confirmation before anyone should trust it.

Gap 4 · Function
Nothing here has been expressed

Every number on this page is a prediction. Stability, fold and purifiability are modelled and not measured, which is what separates a resource from a result.

What is clean

"Clean" here means one specific thing: sequence-level completeness plus independent functional evidence. It is not a claim about every axis, and the distinction matters.

On that definition, the Anaerobic Respiration and Desiccation Resistance sets are fully clean: all 16 candidates land within 90–111% of reference full length, and all 16 carry genuine EC-number evidence. Four cspC and four phrB candidates are clean on every axis we tested, including fold confidence.

Two candidates that earlier drafts of this page called clean are not, and we would rather say so than let the label stand. The two full-length ibpA candidates are ORF-complete but fold at CA-pLDDT 66.9 and 62.6, below our own >80 band; they are complete, not confident. IJEMCG_01450 (recA) has the strongest cross-genome support in the dataset, found in 89 carrier genomes across at least two genera, but it is a 32% ORF fragment at CA-pLDDT 60.5 and sits below the completeness line we drew in Figure 6. Best-attested by provenance is not the same as usable, and it should not have been written in a box labelled clean.

Experimental design: making the screen falsifiable

A screen that is never measured against an outcome stays a heuristic forever. The design below exists to end that. Its first purpose is not to characterise three interesting proteins; it is to produce a measured outcome for enough candidates that the screen acquires a residual, and only then to go deep on the ones that survive.

Everything here is scoped to what a team with one FPLC, a plate reader and a summer can actually run. The expensive part is cloning 22 constructs once.

Tier 1 — Calibration set: 19 candidates, one plate

The 19 constructs are exactly the output of the locked rule in §06: 45 candidates, 26 through Gate 1, 19 through Gate 2. No separate selection was made for this experiment. Those 19 are the calibration set. They span the screen's full predicted range, from 0.515 to 0.940, which is what makes a correlation meaningful; testing only the top three would give three points at one end of the axis and no way to detect that the ranking is wrong.

Small-scale autoinduction in E. coli BL21(DE3), 96-deep-well format, triplicate. Three readouts, each chosen because it maps onto a dimension the screen actually scored:

  • Soluble fraction (soluble ÷ total by SDS-PAGE densitometry) tests the screen as a whole, and is the primary outcome variable.
  • Tm by thermal shift tests the stability dimension against the instability index directly. This is the one dimension carrying 25% of the weight whose input was fitted on 1990-era data.
  • Single-step ion-exchange purity (>90% by gel, no affinity tag) tests the purifiability dimension. 16 of the 19 are predicted tag-free; the 3 phrB candidates with pI 6.4–7.3 are predicted to need a tag, and are the internal negative control for this readout.

Analysis. Spearman ρ between composite score and soluble yield; AUROC for the binary "expressed solubly at all"; and per-dimension partial correlations to find which of the six dimensions carries the signal and which are dead weight. Two baselines must be reported alongside, or the result means nothing: pLDDT alone and instability alone. If the composite does not beat both, the composite is not earning its complexity and we say so.

Control A · the degeneracy probe — built in, costs nothing extra

Eight of the 19 are the Desiccation Resistance set, and all eight score exactly 0.750 on every dimension (§07). The screen cannot distinguish them even in principle. Any spread in their measured outcomes is therefore variance the screen cannot explain, measured directly. That number is the noise floor against which the Tier-1 correlation has to be judged: if the eight identical-score candidates vary as much as the full 0.515–0.940 range does, the ranking carries no information and no refit will save it.

Control B · pre-registered negative prediction — declare before running

The seven membrane candidates found in §07 score 0.71–0.81, above every phrB and both ibpA. We predict, in advance, that all seven fail soluble cytoplasmic expression. They are cloned and run in the same plate under the same protocol.

Writing the prediction down before the experiment is the whole point. If they fail, the screen has a demonstrated, quantified blind spot and a hydrophobicity gate is a justified fix rather than a hunch. If they express, our reading of the annotation was wrong and the screen was right, which we would also have to report. Either way this converts an argument into a result, and it costs seven extra wells.

Sourcing risk that decides the timeline

The genetic assays depend on deletion strains, and they are not equally easy to get. The phr and otsB arms are safe: Keio JW0698 (Δphr) and JW1886 (ΔotsB) are catalogue strains that ship on request [30].

The cspC arm is the problem. BX04, the quadruple ΔcspAΔcspBΔcspEΔcspG strain that makes cold-growth rescue interpretable, is a lab strain with no catalogue entry at ATCC, CGSC or Addgene, and no orderable equivalent exists. It has to be requested from the originating lab or from a group that has published with it, and that request has a lead time we do not control. Since cspC is our flagship candidate, this single logistics item is the most likely reason the strongest arm of this design does not run.

Mitigation: start the strain request now, and treat the in vitro readouts (Tm and beacon melting) as the fallback for cspC. They test biophysics without testing physiology, which is weaker but not nothing. The same applies to phr: the orderable Keio strain is uvrA⁺, so nucleotide excision repair will compress the light-versus-dark window that the assay depends on. Published work uses a phr uvrA double mutant for exactly that reason, and that strain is also lab-request only.

Two conditions that will silently ruin a result

Glycine betaine masks the otsB phenotype. The osmotic-sensitivity of ots mutants is rescued by exogenous betaine [26], so the complementation has to be scored on glucose minimal medium with high NaCl and no betaine. Rich medium will give a clean negative result that means nothing.

SYPRO Orange cannot be used with detergent. If Control B's membrane constructs are ever followed up in detergent or a membrane mimetic, the dye-based thermal shift assay does not transfer; label-free nanoDSF is the alternative, and it needs the protein to contain tryptophan.

What a wet-lab result feeds back

The screen has one calibration parameter it cannot set from sequence data: the weight vector in §06. Every threshold in it comes from literature defaults, and §07 showed at least one category's ranking is sensitive to those defaults at the ±20% level. Tier 1 is designed to end that. With 19 measured outcomes we can fit the weights instead of asserting them, and report a residual.

Three specific things change depending on what comes back, and we would rather commit to them now than decide afterwards:

  1. If the composite beats both single-dimension baselines, the weights get refitted on the measured data and the screen stops being a heuristic. We report the fitted weights next to the hand-set ones so the difference is visible.
  2. If it does not beat pLDDT alone, we drop dimensions until the simpler model wins, and say plainly that five of the six were decoration. A screen that was falsified and then reduced is more useful to the next team than one that was never tested.
  3. If Control B's membrane constructs fail as predicted, a hydrophobicity gate goes in ahead of scoring rather than as a seventh weighted dimension, because the failure is categorical rather than graded. If they express, the gate does not go in and we retract §07's Test 5.

Control A is the reason the desiccation shortlist is worth running even though the screen cannot rank it. Eight candidates with identical predicted properties and different measured outcomes would tell us how much of the outcome the six dimensions never had access to, which is the one number that says whether refitting is worth doing at all.

Contribution, and where the samples came from

A candidate list is a weak contribution. Any team with a cluster can produce one, it is stale the moment the databases move, and nobody can tell from the list which entries to distrust. The three things below are what we think are actually worth handing on.

1. A candidate set that carries its own doubts

The 45 candidates ship with the flags rather than without them: ORF completeness against the canonical ortholog, source-bin CheckM completeness and contamination, pseudogene calls, annotation-conflict status, and EC/PFAM evidence tier. All 53 candidates ever considered are included, the eight excluded ones among them, with the reason each was cut. A team reusing this can see at a glance that phrB is orderable and that the highest-scoring recA is a fragment in a 33.7%-complete bin, which the score alone would never tell them.

2. A checklist for auditing your own dry lab

This is the part we would most want another team to take. Each row below is a check that found a real error in our own output, is cheap to run, and is not specific to desert metagenomes or to stress proteins. Most iGEM dry-lab projects produce a ranked list and stop; none of these checks requires anything beyond what such a project already has.

3. Negative results, stated so others can avoid them

Four failures here are reusable, and none of them is specific to this project. Raw alignment conservation cannot be compared across families of different depth. A hand-weighted score can be perfectly robust and still carry no information, if its inputs are degenerate. A consistency check applied downstream of the filter that guarantees it can never fail. And a physicochemical screen with no hydrophobicity term will rank integral membrane proteins near the top and hand them to a wet lab that cannot express them.

Provenance of the metagenomes

The 1,221 metagenomes analysed here were not generated by this iGEM team. They come from an ongoing Rub' al-Khali microbial ecology survey carried out by the Bio-Ontology Research Group at KAUST, and the survey's own findings on biogeography, soil-position structure and environmental association are reported separately by that group. Our contribution begins downstream of their sampling and assembly: functional mining, candidate prioritisation, and the audit of that prioritisation.

We state this prominently because it changes how the work should be judged. Credit for the sampling campaign and the assemblies belongs to the survey; what is ours to defend is the screening pipeline, the QC-flagged candidate set, and §07.

What still has to happen before this counts as a contribution

An honest availability section is not the same as an available dataset. To make this usable by another team we still owe: an OSI licence on the code, an archived DOI for the candidate tables and QC supplement, an environment specification so versions are pinned, and a parameters table covering every threshold, random seed, resampling N and the Dirichlet concentration parameter. Five of the nine tools in Table 2 currently show no recorded version, and no database release versions are recorded at all, which means the GTDB coverage figure in §09 is not reproducible even in principle.
